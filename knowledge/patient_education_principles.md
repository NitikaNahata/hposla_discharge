# Patient Education and Teach-Back Principles

SYNTHETIC reference generated for this capstone. Not a clinical guideline.

## Reading level

Discharge instructions should target roughly a 6th-grade reading level. Use short sentences and
common words. Prefer "water pill" to "diuretic", "blood thinner" to "anticoagulant", and "an
infection in your lungs" to "pneumonia". Naming the clinical term once alongside the plain
description is useful, since the patient will encounter it elsewhere, but the plain phrase should
lead.

## Red-flag symptoms must be concrete

Abstract warnings such as "return if symptoms worsen" are not actionable and are reliably ignored.
Red flags must be observable and specific: "you gain more than 4 pounds in 2 days", "your ankles
swell", "you need more than two pillows to sleep", "your bleeding does not stop after 10 minutes
of pressure". Each red flag should state what to do as well as what to notice.

## Teach-back

Teach-back is asking the patient to explain the instruction back in their own words, not asking
whether they understood. "Can you tell me how you will take this water pill?" is teach-back;
"Do you understand?" is not. A patient who cannot state their own diuretic dose after teach-back
should not be discharged without a dosette box, a caregiver briefing, or a follow-up telephone
check within 72 hours.

## Caregivers and living alone

Where a patient lives alone and has no identified caregiver, the education packet must be written
so that it stands on its own without assuming anyone will help, and the follow-up plan must
compensate with earlier contact. Where a caregiver exists, the packet should carry a separate
short caregiver section stating what specifically to watch for.

## Language

Where the patient's primary language is not English, the packet should be produced in that
language and a professional interpreter used for teach-back. Family members should not be used as
interpreters for discharge instructions.
