# Medication Reconciliation Standards at Discharge

SYNTHETIC reference generated for this capstone. Not a clinical guideline.

## The core requirement

Every medication on the pre-admission list must be explicitly accounted for at discharge as
continued, stopped, started or modified. A medication that simply disappears from the list without
a recorded decision is the most common reconciliation defect and a genuine patient-safety event,
because patients routinely resume medications at home that a clinician had silently intended to
stop.

Where a decision cannot be made confidently, the medication should be recorded as unreconciled and
flagged for the receiving clinician. Reporting uncertainty is far more useful than a confident
guess.

## Documentation of stopped medications

When a medication is stopped, the reason must be recorded in terms the patient can understand and
repeated in the education packet. "Ibuprofen has been stopped because it makes your heart failure
worse and can damage your kidneys. Use acetaminophen for your knee pain instead" is adequate.
"Ibuprofen discontinued" is not.

## High-alert medications

Anticoagulants, insulin, opioids, and medications with a narrow therapeutic index warrant
additional scrutiny at discharge. Any change to a high-alert medication should trigger explicit
monitoring instructions and an earlier follow-up interval than would otherwise apply.

## Allergy cross-check

The discharge medication list must be cross-checked against documented allergies as a distinct
step. An overlap between a documented allergy and a discharge medication requires pharmacist
review before dispensing, without exception.

## Pharmacist review triggers

Pharmacist review is required before dispensing when any interaction is of major or
contraindicated severity, when a documented allergy overlaps the discharge list, when more than
one high-alert medication has been changed, or when the patient is discharged on more than ten
regular medications.
