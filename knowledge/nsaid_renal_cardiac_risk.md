# NSAID Risk in Heart Failure and Chronic Kidney Disease

SYNTHETIC reference generated for this capstone. Not a clinical guideline.

## Why NSAIDs matter at discharge

Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs are among the most frequently overlooked medications during
reconciliation, because patients often buy them over the counter and do not think of them as
medication. In heart failure and chronic kidney disease they are a common and preventable cause
of readmission.

NSAIDs cause renal prostaglandin inhibition, which reduces renal perfusion, promotes sodium and
water retention, and blunts the natriuretic effect of loop diuretics. In a patient on furosemide
this can precipitate decompensation directly.

## The triple whammy

The combination of an NSAID, a diuretic, and a renin-angiotensin system blocker such as an ACE
inhibitor, an angiotensin receptor blocker, or sacubitril-valsartan is known as the triple whammy
and carries a markedly elevated risk of acute kidney injury. Any patient discharged on both a
diuretic and a RAAS agent should have NSAIDs explicitly stopped and documented as stopped.

## Substitution

Acetaminophen is the analgesic of choice in heart failure and chronic kidney disease. Doses up to
3 grams per day are generally appropriate for musculoskeletal pain such as osteoarthritis. Topical
NSAIDs carry substantially lower systemic exposure and are a reasonable alternative for localised
joint pain where oral acetaminophen is insufficient.

## Reconciliation practice

When an NSAID is stopped during an admission, the reason must be recorded explicitly in the
discharge summary and stated in plain language in the patient education packet. A medication that
disappears from the list without explanation is frequently restarted by the patient at home,
which reproduces the original problem.
